A Scottsdale homeowner pays the city $400 a month in summer water bills to keep a fescue front lawn alive that nobody walks on. The city, meanwhile, would pay that same homeowner $1,500 to rip the lawn out and plant something that survives the desert. The math has been like this for over a decade. Most homeowners still pay the bill.

Arizona has had xeriscape protections written into state law since 1986 — the term "xeriscape" was actually coined in Denver but Arizona was one of the first states to legally protect a homeowner's right to install one. The Arizona Municipal Water Users Association (AMWUA) has been running turf removal rebate programs across the Phoenix metro since the late 1990s. The legal framework, the rebate cash, and the seed selection are all available. What's missing, mostly, is awareness.

This guide is the missing piece.

## Who Pays What: AMWUA Cities vs. Tucson

The first thing to understand is which water provider you have, because that determines which rebate program applies.

**AMWUA member cities:**
- Phoenix
- Mesa
- Scottsdale
- Tempe
- Gilbert
- Chandler
- Glendale
- Peoria

Each AMWUA city runs its own turf removal rebate program with rates and rules that vary by city, but they share common application infrastructure and approved-plant lists.

**Tucson and Pima County are NOT in AMWUA.** Tucson Water runs its own separate Smart Rebate program with different rates, different application paperwork, and a different inspector pool. If you live in Tucson, Oro Valley, Marana, or unincorporated Pima County, you're in the Tucson Water service area or one of the smaller East Valley districts. Confirm on your water bill before applying anywhere.

Typical 2026 rebate rates by city:

| City | Residential Rebate | Cap |
|---|---|---|
| Phoenix | $0.50/sq ft | 10,000 sq ft |
| Mesa | $0.25–$0.50/sq ft | varies |
| Scottsdale | $1.50/sq ft | 5,000 sq ft (premium tier program) |
| Tempe | $0.50/sq ft | 10,000 sq ft |
| Glendale | $0.50/sq ft | 5,000 sq ft |
| Chandler | $0.20/sq ft | program-specific |
| Tucson Water | $0.50/sq ft | 10,000 sq ft |

The numbers look smaller than California or Nevada, but Arizona water is also cheaper, and the rebate is stacked on top of multi-thousand-dollar annual savings on water bills. A 3,000 sq ft front-yard conversion in Scottsdale at $1.50/sq ft is $4,500 in your hand plus typically $1,200–$2,000/year in water savings.

## Arizona's HOA Xeriscape Protection: ARS § 33-1816 and § 33-441

Two state statutes protect homeowner rights to install drought-tolerant landscaping:

**ARS § 33-1816** (planned communities — most modern subdivisions and HOAs): "Notwithstanding any provision in the community documents, an association shall not prohibit the use of vegetable gardens or xeriscaping on a member's property." The statute goes further to clarify that HOAs can establish reasonable design guidelines for xeriscape installations, but cannot block them outright.

**ARS § 33-441** (broader real estate provisions): Prohibits restrictions that prevent water-efficient landscaping techniques, including xeriscape design, on residential property in Arizona.

In plain language: your HOA cannot stop you from xeriscaping. They can require that the design meets reasonable aesthetic standards (no half-finished projects, no dead plants, edges defined, irrigation hidden) — but they cannot say "no."

If you're getting pushback from your HOA, the path is straightforward:

1. Submit a complete xeriscape plan with plant list, layout, and a maintenance schedule
2. Cite ARS § 33-1816 (planned community) or § 33-441 (other) in writing
3. Reference the AMWUA approved-plant list to demonstrate the design uses recognized xeric species
4. Request written response from the board

Most HOAs back down at this stage. The few that don't are exposed to legal challenge — Arizona courts have consistently sided with homeowners on xeriscape disputes when the homeowner's plan meets reasonable design standards.

## The Climate Split: Low Desert vs. High Desert

Arizona looks like one big desert on the map, but the state spans elevations from roughly 100 feet to over 12,000 feet. What grows in Phoenix will die in Flagstaff, and vice versa. The seed strategy depends on which Arizona you're in.

### Low Desert (Phoenix Metro, Tucson, Yuma)

Elevation under 3,000 feet. Summer highs of 110–115°F. Mild winters where freezes are uncommon and rarely sustained. Soils are alkaline, often rocky, with caliche layers that block drainage.

**Recommended species:**

- **[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/)** — The default low-desert lawn. Handles 115°F afternoons, recovers from foot traffic, and uses about a third of the water Kentucky bluegrass demands. Goes dormant brown from roughly mid-November through mid-March. Some homeowners overseed with annual ryegrass for winter green, but most learn to embrace the dormant brown — it's how everything else in the desert looks too.
- **[Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix](/products/sonoran-desert-wildflower-mix/)** — Mexican gold poppy, desert marigold, blanketflower, owl's clover, lupine. Plant in fall for spring bloom. Critical for HOA front-yard applications because it provides the "color" that boards often want to see in approved plans.
- **[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/)** — Works in low desert as a transition-zone accent grass. Taller than Buffalograss (18–24 inches), useful along fences, near patios, and as the "soft" element in a landscape that's otherwise rock and shrub.

A common low-desert layout: Bermudagrass for the main lawn area where the dog runs, Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix for slopes and parkways, decomposed granite around foundation plantings, drought-tolerant shrubs (creosote, brittlebush, palo verde, mesquite) for vertical structure.

### High Desert (Flagstaff, Prescott, Sedona, Show Low)

Elevation 4,500–7,000 feet. Summer highs in the 80s. Winters with sustained freezes, snow, and temperatures into the single digits. Soils still tend to be alkaline but with more organic matter than low-desert sites.

**Recommended species:**

- **[Blue Grama](/products/blue-grama/)** — The native short grass of the Colorado Plateau. Handles freeze cycles, drought, and the high-elevation UV stress that breaks lower-elevation species. Fine-textured, soft, with the distinctive eyelash seed heads in fall.
- **[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/)** — Works at high elevation as a structural bunchgrass. Particularly good in transition zones between maintained landscape and native areas.
- **Buffalograss** is borderline at the upper end of high-desert elevations — it tolerates cold but doesn't love the shorter growing season. [Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) does well in Prescott (5,400 ft) but struggles above 6,500 ft. Below 5,500 ft it's a strong option.

A common high-desert layout: Blue Grama as the main groundcover lawn, Sideoats Grama as accent, native wildflowers (Rocky Mountain penstemon, blanketflower, Indian paintbrush) for color. Pinyon, juniper, and Gambel oak for vertical structure.

## When to Seed in Arizona

Timing windows are narrow and unforgiving. The state's intense sun and arid air mean seed has to germinate and root fast or fail.

**Low desert:**
- Spring: March 15 – April 30. Soil temps reach 60°F+, daytime highs are still under 95°F.
- Fall: September 1 – October 15. After monsoon season, before freezes.
- Avoid: May–August (too hot for seedlings to survive), late November–February (too cold for warm-season germination).

**High desert:**
- Spring: April 15 – May 31. Wait for snowmelt and soil temps to reach 55°F+.
- Fall: August 15 – September 30. Use late-summer monsoon moisture for establishment.
- Avoid: November–March (frozen ground, dormancy).

For wildflower mixes in both zones, fall is the strongly preferred window. Wildflower seeds need cold winter conditions to break dormancy and germinate properly when spring temperatures rise.

## How to Apply for an AMWUA Rebate

AMWUA cities have a roughly common application flow, but each city handles its own portal and inspector visits.

**General process:**
1. Pre-inspection or pre-approval: most cities require photos and a plan submission before demo
2. Approval letter or work order issued
3. Demo existing turf — herbicide kill, sod cut, or solarize
4. Install approved replacement landscape (seed, hardscape, drip irrigation, plants)
5. Establishment period (typically 60–90 days for seed to develop coverage)
6. Submit completion paperwork with final photos
7. Inspector visits to verify
8. Payment in 4–8 weeks

**City-specific variations:**

- **Phoenix:** apply through phoenix.gov/waterservices, Landscape Conversion Rebate. Allows DIY or contractor installations.
- **Mesa:** mesaaz.gov/residents/water-resources, Grass to Xeriscape Conversion. Pre-inspection required.
- **Scottsdale:** scottsdaleaz.gov/water — runs the most generous rebate per square foot of any AMWUA city. Strict design review.
- **Tempe:** tempe.gov/water, Landscape Rebate Program. Generally homeowner-friendly process.
- **Glendale:** glendaleaz.com/water — straightforward application, typical AMWUA process.
- **Tucson Water (separate program):** tucsonaz.gov/water, Smart Rebate. Photographic documentation throughout, no pre-approval required for residential under a threshold.

## Documentation Requirements

Across AMWUA cities and Tucson Water, the documentation expectations are similar:

- Pre-conversion photos showing existing turf
- Square footage measurements
- Planting plan (handwritten sketch is fine for residential)
- List of plant species with quantities
- Irrigation layout (most programs require drip conversion)
- Receipts for materials (sometimes required)
- Post-conversion photos at maturity

Inspectors are looking for: 50%+ live plant coverage at maturity (the AMWUA standard), no remaining turf, irrigation converted from sprinkler to drip, plants from the approved list, edges clean and defined.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

A 2,500 sq ft Phoenix front yard converted from fescue to a mix of Common Bermudagrass (1,800 sq ft active lawn area), Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix (400 sq ft beds and parkway), and decomposed granite with drought-tolerant shrubs (300 sq ft):

- Seed cost: ~$280
- Drip irrigation conversion: ~$400
- Decomposed granite: ~$200
- Shrubs and trees: ~$300
- Total out-of-pocket: ~$1,180
- Phoenix rebate: 2,500 × $0.50 = $1,250
- Annual water savings: ~$800–$1,200

Net positive in year one, and meaningfully positive every year after.

## Why Most Arizona Homeowners Haven't Done This

The honest answer is that the rebate amounts in Arizona look small compared to the California and Nevada numbers in the news. People hear "$2 per square foot in Vegas" and assume their state isn't worth bothering with. They miss that:

1. Arizona water is cheap because of the Central Arizona Project, but the same drought pressures are eroding that — water rates are rising 5–8% annually across the Phoenix metro
2. The rebate plus annual water bill savings makes the math work even at $0.50/sq ft
3. The HOA legal protections in Arizona are stronger than in most Western states
4. Climate change projections show the low desert getting hotter and drier — turf will become more expensive to maintain, not less
