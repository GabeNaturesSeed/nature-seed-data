The bag of grass seed at the Home Depot in Rio Rancho was bred for Indianapolis. The picture on the front shows a green lawn under a hardwood tree in summer — a scene that does not exist in New Mexico. The seed is mostly Kentucky bluegrass, perennial ryegrass, and tall fescue. It is engineered for 38 inches of annual rainfall, deep silty topsoil, and humid summers. Albuquerque gets 9 inches of rain in a good year, sits at 5,300 feet, and runs 95°F in July with 8% humidity.

Plant that bag of seed and you'll spend three years and several thousand dollars trying to get a lawn to survive on three times the water the climate provides, on soil it didn't evolve for, in UV that fries it from May through September. Most homeowners give up before year three. The lawn is dead. The water bill was paid the whole time.

New Mexico is 98% arid or semi-arid. Almost nothing on the big-box garden center shelf is right for it. The seed that *is* right is grown specifically for high-desert conditions and, when matched to the elevation and climate of your part of the state, will establish in one season and run on rainfall plus minimal supplemental water for the rest of its life.

This guide covers the plants that work, the rebate programs that pay you to use them, and the elevation differences that make Albuquerque a different planting problem from Santa Fe.

## ABCWUA Xeriscape Program (Albuquerque)

The Albuquerque Bernalillo County Water Utility Authority runs one of the longest-standing cash-for-grass programs in the Southwest. ABCWUA's xeriscape rebate is available to both residential and commercial customers in the service area, and it is structured around removal of high-water turf and replacement with water-wise landscaping that meets the agency's approved plant list.

The basic flow:

1. **Pre-inspection.** Apply before any grass is removed. ABCWUA sends an inspector to confirm the existing turf area, square footage, and irrigation. If you remove first and apply later, you don't get paid.
2. **Plan submission.** You submit a plan showing the new landscape design, including species, plant spacing, and irrigation conversion (drip is typically required for new plantings, no overhead spray on the converted area).
3. **Approved plant list.** ABCWUA maintains a published plant list. Replacement species must come from that list to qualify. Most native and adapted Southwest species are on it — including the seed-based grasses and wildflower mixes covered below — but check the current list before you finalize the plan.
4. **Installation and post-inspection.** After the work is done, ABCWUA inspects again to confirm the converted area meets coverage requirements. The rebate pays out per square foot of qualifying conversion.

Rates and per-property caps change. Check abcwua.org for the current per-square-foot rebate and the maximum payout per property before you build your budget around a specific number.

For commercial property managers in Albuquerque, the ABCWUA rebate combined with the long-term irrigation savings on a converted property typically pays back the conversion in two to four years, depending on the size and the water use of the original turf.

## Santa Fe Water Division

Santa Fe runs a separate program. The Santa Fe Water Division has historically offered rebates for turf removal and conversion to water-wise landscaping, and the city's broader water plan treats drought-resistant landscaping as part of the official conservation strategy — not an optional aesthetic.

Santa Fe's per-capita water use is among the lowest of any U.S. city, and that didn't happen by accident. The city has been pushing customers toward xeriscape for two decades through tiered water rates that punish heavy outdoor use, ordinances on new construction landscaping, and rebate dollars for retrofits. If you live inside Santa Fe city limits, your water bill already reflects the policy. Converting your yard to a water-wise design is mostly about getting out from under the top-tier rate.

Check the Santa Fe Water Division for current program rules and approved-species lists. Santa Fe's program has often required pre-approval and may have annual funding caps.

## Elevation Zones in New Mexico

This is where most generic "xeriscape" advice falls apart for New Mexico homeowners. The state spans elevations from about 2,800 feet (in the southeast desert) to over 13,000 feet in the Sangre de Cristo range. Two of the largest population centers — Albuquerque and Santa Fe — sit nearly 1,700 feet apart in elevation, and that difference shows up in winter low temperatures, soil temperature in spring, and which plants establish reliably.

**Albuquerque (~5,300 ft) and Las Cruces (~3,900 ft): high desert, arid.** Hot summers, mild winters by Mountain West standards, very low rainfall, sandy or sandy-loam soils with caliche layers in some areas. Warm-season natives dominate. This is Buffalograss country, Blue Grama country, and Bermudagrass country in the lower Rio Grande Valley.

Seeding windows: spring April 15 – May 15 (after soil hits 60°F, before the worst of the May heat); fall September 1 – 15 (warm soil, cooling air, before the early-October frost risk for Albuquerque — Las Cruces gets a longer fall window).

**Santa Fe (~7,000 ft) and Taos (~7,000 ft): semi-arid, colder winters.** Real freeze-thaw cycles. Snow in winter. Cooler summers than Albuquerque. Native shortgrasses still work, but the warm-climate options (like common Bermudagrass) won't survive winter at this elevation. The mix shifts toward cold-tolerant Buffalograss varieties, Blue Grama, and a higher proportion of cool-season options.

Seeding windows: spring May 1 – June 1 (later than Albuquerque, after hard frost risk passes and soil warms); fall by August 31 (early frost risk in September means seedlings need extra growing time before dormancy).

If you're in between — Los Alamos, Las Vegas NM, Ruidoso, the Gila country — split the difference based on your specific microclimate. Soil temperature is the better signal than the calendar.

## Species for New Mexico

**Blue Grama** ([/products/blue-grama/](/products/blue-grama/)) is the finest native turf grass in New Mexico, full stop. It's adapted from the Albuquerque heat to the Santa Fe cold, handles alkaline soils, runs on rainfall in most years, and produces the iconic eyebrow-shaped seed head that defines a Western prairie. Mature height is 6–12 inches if left unmowed; mow to 3–4 inches for a more conventional turf appearance. It's warm-season — green from late spring through early fall, tan in winter — and goes through a brief dormancy in mid-summer if rainfall fails before the monsoon arrives, then greens back up.

For a homeowner converting a Kentucky bluegrass lawn in Albuquerque, Blue Grama is usually 80% of the answer. Establish it once. Water lightly through year one. After that, supplemental irrigation drops to a fraction of what bluegrass demands.

**Sideoats Grama** ([/products/sideoats-grama/](/products/sideoats-grama/)) is the taller cousin — 18–30 inches at maturity, with the distinctive flag-like seed heads that hang off one side of the stem. Sideoats works as a structural element in xeriscape designs: along property lines, on slopes that need erosion control, in transition zones between mowed lawn and native plantings. It's not a substitute for Blue Grama in a turf application, but it's the right grass to fill out a meadow-style front yard alongside Blue Grama and wildflowers.

**Southwest Wildflower Mix** ([/products/southwest-wildflower-mix/](/products/southwest-wildflower-mix/)) provides the spring and summer color most homeowners want from a xeriscape. The mix is built from species adapted to Southwest conditions — Mexican hat, blanketflower, prairie coneflower, plains coreopsis, desert marigold, others — and produces staggered bloom from late spring into fall. Year one tilts toward the annuals; year two and beyond, the perennials and reseeders dominate. After establishment, water is supplemental at most.

This mix qualifies on most municipal approved plant lists in New Mexico because the species composition is regionally appropriate.

**Buffalograss** ([/products/buffalograss/](/products/buffalograss/)) is excellent for Albuquerque, Rio Rancho, the Lower Rio Grande, and Las Cruces. Cold-hardy varieties handle freeze-thaw without trouble. Fine-bladed, blue-green, dormant in winter, and often blended with Blue Grama for full-season coverage. Mature height 4–8 inches if unmowed.

**One important elevation note: Buffalograss is not recommended above 6,500 feet.** The growing season at Santa Fe and Taos elevations is too short for reliable establishment, and winter kill becomes a real risk. If you're in Santa Fe, Los Alamos, or Taos, lean on Blue Grama as the primary turf-replacement species and skip Buffalograss.

## What to Avoid: Tall Fescue at the Big-Box Store

Walk into any Home Depot, Lowe's, or Walmart in Albuquerque and you will find bags of tall fescue blends marketed as "drought-tolerant" lawn seed. Compared to Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue does use less water. Compared to Blue Grama or Buffalograss in New Mexico conditions, tall fescue uses two to three times more.

Tall fescue is a cool-season grass bred for the transition zone — the band of states from Virginia through Missouri where summers are hot but humid and winters are mild. It is *not* a desert grass. In Albuquerque, tall fescue requires regular irrigation through the summer to avoid burning out, struggles with alkaline soils common to the Rio Grande Valley, and offers no advantage over Blue Grama for any xeriscape application.

If you are converting an existing tall fescue lawn, you are making the same mistake the previous owner made. Tear it out, prep the soil, and seed natives. The "drought-tolerant" marketing on the bag was a comparative claim against bluegrass, not a recommendation for the high desert.

## Pulling It Together

For most homeowners, the right play in New Mexico is a Blue Grama base with a Southwest Wildflower Mix in the non-turf areas, plus Sideoats Grama or Buffalograss as supporting species depending on elevation. Apply for the ABCWUA or Santa Fe rebate before any work begins. Convert irrigation to drip on the planting beds and remove or cap the spray heads on the former turf area. Seed in the right window for your elevation. Water through year one to establish. After that, the landscape mostly takes care of itself.
