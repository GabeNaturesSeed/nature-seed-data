Somewhere along Arapahoe Road there is a shopping center with a 4,000-square-foot strip of Kentucky bluegrass between the parking lot and the sidewalk. Nobody walks on it. Nobody picnics on it. The sprinklers come on at 4 a.m. four times a week to keep it green from May to October, and the property manager has never once questioned the line item on the water bill because that grass has been there since 1998 and that is what shopping centers look like.

Starting in 2026, that strip of grass is illegal.

Colorado's Senate Bill 24-005 phased in a ban on "non-functional turf" for commercial, institutional, and HOA common-area properties. Governmental and commercial landscape installers cannot install non-functional cool-season turf as of 2026, and existing non-functional turf at large public and commercial sites is being phased out under a longer compliance schedule. Most commercial property managers we talk to have not heard of it. Their landscape contractors haven't told them. The water utilities haven't sent a letter. They are about to discover the new rule the hard way — through a violation notice, a denied permit, or a lease tenant who reads the news and asks why the building is out of compliance.

If you manage commercial real estate, an HOA common area, or a municipal streetscape in Colorado, this is your problem now. If you're a homeowner who's been told by an HOA that you can't xeriscape, the law changed for you too — in 2023.

## HB23-1233: Your HOA Cannot Block Xeriscaping Anymore

In 2023 the Colorado legislature passed HB23-1233, signed into law by Governor Polis. The statute does two things that matter to homeowners in HOA-governed neighborhoods:

1. **HOAs must offer at least three water-wise landscaping designs** that homeowners can choose from. Not one token "rock garden" option buried in the appendix of the architectural guidelines. Three real, distinct, pre-approved designs.
2. **HOAs cannot prohibit xeriscaping outright.** Any covenant, rule, or guideline that effectively bans drought-tolerant landscaping is unenforceable.

What this means in practice: if your HOA's CC&Rs say you must maintain "lush green turf" or that "no more than 25% of the front yard may be non-turf," those provisions can no longer be used to block a xeriscape. The HOA still has design review authority — they can require clean edges, defined planting areas, and a maintenance plan. They cannot say no to the underlying concept.

The standard playbook when an HOA pushes back:

1. Ask the HOA in writing for the three approved water-wise designs the law requires them to offer. Many HOAs in Front Range suburbs still don't have them on file. The request alone moves the conversation.
2. Submit your xeriscape plan as a request to use one of those approved designs (or a close variation). Frame it as compliance with HB23-1233, not a fight.
3. If the HOA refuses to approve any design, you have a statutory complaint. Most boards back down before it gets there because their attorneys have told them they will lose.

A side note for board members reading this: the statute also gives you cover. If a vocal minority of homeowners is fighting the change, you can point to state law and stop being the bad guy. That's worth something.

## The 2026 Commercial Non-Functional Turf Ban

SB24-005 is the bigger structural change, and it has not been on most property managers' radar. Here's what it covers in plain English:

**What's banned:**
- Ornamental turf on commercial properties (office parks, retail centers, hotels, industrial properties)
- HOA common areas — the strips of grass nobody actually uses, medians inside subdivisions, entrance monuments
- Municipal streetscapes, medians, and ornamental areas around public buildings
- New installations by governmental entities and commercial landscape installers as of 2026

**What's exempt — "functional turf":**
- Sports fields and athletic facilities
- Playgrounds and active-use park areas
- Cemeteries
- Golf course playing surfaces (fairways, greens, tees, roughs)
- Areas used for events, picnics, or active recreation

The line between functional and non-functional is the test. If people regularly walk, sit, play, or recreate on the grass, it's functional. If it exists to be looked at, it's non-functional, and after the compliance dates it has to come out.

**What this means for commercial property managers:**

The grass strip between your parking lot and the sidewalk has to go. The decorative lawn at your building's entrance has to go. The HOA common area where nobody plays has to go. You'll need to budget for removal and replacement on every property in your portfolio that has non-functional cool-season turf.

The replacement options that actually work in Colorado's climate are not Astroturf, not gravel deserts, and not ornamental grasses imported from California. The options that work are native and adapted seed mixes that establish in one or two seasons, then run on rainfall and a fraction of the irrigation. We'll cover the species below.

**For HOA boards:** start the conversation with your management company now. Inventory the non-functional turf in your common areas. Get bids on conversion. The longer you wait, the higher the demand will be on landscape contractors who can do this work, and the more your assessments will spike when you have to pay rush prices in 2027.

## Denver Water Cash-for-Grass and Other Front Range Rebates

Denver Water runs the largest cash-for-grass program in the state. Homeowners and businesses can apply for a rebate on the square footage of irrigated cool-season turf removed and replaced with low-water landscaping. Rates change — Denver Water has revised its rebate amounts more than once in the last few years, and the program is sometimes oversubscribed and capped for the season — so check current rates and program status at denverwater.org before budgeting.

The basics of how it works:

- Apply *before* you remove the grass. Pre-inspection is required. If you tear out the lawn first and apply afterward, the rebate is denied.
- The replacement landscape must meet a plant-coverage and water-use threshold. Bare gravel doesn't qualify. Living plants, native or adapted, do.
- Approved species lists are published. Most of the seed-based options below qualify.
- Post-installation inspection happens after planting. The rebate pays out after that inspection.

Beyond Denver Water, the rebate landscape on the Front Range includes:

- **Aurora Water** — separate cash-for-grass program with its own approved plant list and per-square-foot rate. Aurora has been one of the more aggressive municipal programs in the state.
- **Castle Rock Water** — turf replacement rebate program for residents of the Town of Castle Rock; tends to have strong seasonal funding.
- **Colorado Springs Utilities** — landscape rebates available; check current program rules.
- Several smaller utilities (Westminster, Greeley, Fort Collins) run their own incentives. If you're outside Denver Water's service area, search your utility's name plus "turf rebate" — the program almost certainly exists.

The **CWCB Turf Replacement Grant** at the state level was paused as of March 2025 and is not currently accepting applications, so don't plan around it.

## Species That Actually Work on the Front Range

The Front Range climate — cold winters, dry summers, freeze-thaw cycles in spring, high-altitude UV, alkaline clay soils in many areas — is hostile to most of what's sold at big-box garden centers. The species below have been bred or adapted for these conditions.

**Blue Grama** ([/products/blue-grama/](/products/blue-grama/)) is the foundation species for almost every Colorado xeriscape we recommend. It's a warm-season native shortgrass with fine blades, the iconic "eyebrow" seed head, and a mature height of 6–12 inches if left unmowed. It greens up in late May, holds through summer on rainfall in most of the Front Range, and goes tan-dormant in winter. It handles freeze-thaw, alkaline soils, and the dry, hot July days in Lakewood or Aurora that fry Kentucky bluegrass. Seed in late May through June when soil temperature reaches 55°F.

**Buffalograss** ([/products/buffalograss/](/products/buffalograss/)) is the other native shortgrass. It's a true companion to Blue Grama and is often seeded as a blend. **Important:** for Colorado, you want cold-hardy varieties — Cody and Bowie are the two we typically use. The warm-climate Buffalograss varieties (Prestige, Legacy) bred for Texas and the Southern Plains do not survive Colorado winters reliably. Same caution applies to common Bermudagrass: it's appropriate for southern climates and will winter-kill on the Front Range. Don't let a contractor talk you into Bermudagrass for a Lakewood lawn. Plant it once, lose it once, and remember.

**Sheep Fescue** ([/products/sheep-fescue-grass/](/products/sheep-fescue-grass/)) is the cool-season counterpart to Blue Grama and Buffalograss. It stays green spring and fall when the warm-season natives are dormant, and it tolerates partial shade — which makes it the right pick for north-facing slopes, areas under tree canopy, and shaded HOA common areas where Blue Grama struggles. Lower water than Kentucky bluegrass by a wide margin. Can be left unmowed for a meadow look or mowed at 3–4 inches for a more conventional turf.

**Microclover** ([/products/micro-clover-seed-2/](/products/micro-clover-seed-2/)) is the option for homeowners who want something that *looks* like a lawn — green, soft, walkable — without the water bill. Micro-clover fixes nitrogen (so it fertilizes itself), stays low without mowing, and works either as a standalone groundcover or blended into a fescue or bluegrass lawn at 5–10% by weight to reduce overall water need. It's HOA-friendly because most board members can't tell it isn't grass at a glance.

**Rocky Mountain Wildflower Mix** ([/products/rocky-mountain-wildflower-mix/](/products/rocky-mountain-wildflower-mix/)) is for non-turf areas — slopes, parking strips, the area along the back fence where you don't need a lawn. The mix combines native perennials and reseeding annuals adapted to the Rocky Mountain region. Year one is mostly annuals; year two and beyond, the perennials dominate. After establishment, water is supplemental at most.

For larger commercial conversions, a Blue Grama and Buffalograss blend with Sheep Fescue mixed in for shaded zones — plus pockets of Rocky Mountain Wildflower Mix at entry points and along walkways for color — produces a finished landscape that meets most municipal approved-plant-list requirements and qualifies for major rebate programs.

## When to Seed in Colorado

Two windows work, and they're tighter than people think.

**Late spring (May 15 – June 30):** wait until after the last hard frost — typically May 10–15 along the Front Range, later at higher elevations. Soil temperature should be 55°F or above for warm-season natives to germinate reliably. Seeding too early leaves the seed sitting in cold ground where it rots or gets eaten.

**Late summer (August):** soil is warm, evening temperatures are dropping, and there's still enough growing season before the first fall frost (around September 15 in Denver, earlier at higher elevation) for the seedlings to harden off. This window works well for cool-season species like Sheep Fescue.

Avoid mid-summer seeding (July) — the heat and dryness make germination unreliable without daily irrigation. Avoid late fall — seedlings won't have time to root before winter kills them.

For dormant seeding (broadcasting seed in late November or December onto frozen ground so it overwinters and germinates with spring snowmelt), Blue Grama and Buffalograss can work in some sites, but it's a higher-risk play. Stick with the two main windows unless you have experience.
