Your June water bill is the highest in the neighborhood, the lawn still looks patchy, and your landscaper says it needs more nitrogen. The problem isn't you — it's Kentucky bluegrass, a humid-climate turf trying to survive at 5,280 feet in a semi-arid climate that gets fifteen inches of precipitation a year. You bought a lawn species the climate doesn't support.

The fix is the native seed that was on the Front Range before bluegrass arrived: Blue Grama, cold-hardy Buffalograss, Sheep Fescue, Rocky Mountain Wildflower Mix. Adapted to the climate, the alkaline soils, the freeze-thaw cycles, and the high-altitude UV that breaks imported species. Denver Water and several Front Range neighbors will pay you to make the switch, and the species below qualify on the approved-replacement lists.

## Approved Replacement Species — Front Range Lineup

### Blue Grama — the foundation native

[Blue Grama](/products/blue-grama/) is the headline native grass for Front Range conversions. It's a fine-bladed, warm-season prairie grass native to the short-grass steppe that runs from the foothills east through the plains. Mature plants tolerate full Colorado summer drought without supplemental water once established and handle the freeze-thaw cycles that wreck a lot of imported species.

The blue-green color reads as a lawn from a distance; up close it has the characteristic eyebrow-shaped seed heads. Mature unmowed height is 6–12 inches; mow at 3–4 inches for a more conventional turf appearance. Greens up in late May, runs through summer on rainfall in most of the Front Range, goes tan-dormant in winter (matches the surrounding foothills).

Plant May 15 to June 30, when soil temperatures hit 55°F. Plan on 60 days minimum before rebate inspection.

### Buffalograss — Critical Note for Colorado

[Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) is the other major Front Range native, and the warning here matters: **only cold-hardy varieties — Cody or Bowie — survive Colorado winters**. Warm-climate Buffalograss varieties marketed for Texas, Oklahoma, and the southern plains will not make it through a Denver winter. They'll establish fine the first summer and die in the first hard freeze.

Confirm the variety before ordering — the Nature's Seed Buffalograss product page lists which varieties are stocked. Buying a southern variety is the single most common cause of failed Front Range conversions.

Why cold-hardy buffalograss works: native to the high plains, evolved through the same freeze-thaw cycles and dry continental climate that defines the Front Range. Once established, it asks for almost nothing — a deep watering once or twice a month is plenty. Soft, fine-bladed, dormant tan in winter. Plant May 15 to June 30. Slow to establish, drought-tolerant once mature.

### Sheep Fescue — cool-season counterpart

[Sheep Fescue Grass](/products/sheep-fescue-grass/) stays green spring and fall when Blue Grama and Buffalograss are dormant or warming up, and greens up early in spring. It tolerates partial shade — north-facing slopes, areas under tree canopy — where Blue Grama struggles. Lower water than Kentucky bluegrass by a wide margin.

Why it works here: drought-tolerant cool-season bunchgrass that handles freeze-thaw and the alkaline clay soils common along the Front Range. Often the right pick for shaded portions of a yard where Blue Grama would thin out. A common Front Range approach is Blue Grama in sunny areas and Sheep Fescue in shaded patches — the two coexist and look unified at lawn distance.

### Rocky Mountain Wildflower Mix — non-turf areas

[Rocky Mountain Wildflower Mix](/products/rocky-mountain-wildflower-mix/) is the companion planting for any structural-grass conversion. Native perennials and reseeding annuals adapted to the Rocky Mountain region fill the gaps between bunchgrasses with color, support pollinators, and read as intentional landscape design rather than abandoned lot. Year one tilts toward the annuals; year two and beyond, the perennials dominate.

Use it in border bands, around boulders, and in the transition zone between structural grass and shrub beds. Most rebate programs accept wildflower areas as part of the converted square footage as long as cover thresholds are met at inspection.

## Seeding Windows for the Front Range

- **Late spring (May 15 – June 30):** Primary window for warm-season natives — Blue Grama, Buffalograss, native wildflowers. Soil is finally warm enough for germination.
- **Late summer (August 1 – August 25):** Secondary window for cool-season species like Sheep Fescue. Avoid seeding past August 25 in the Denver area — September frost risk is real.
- **Avoid early spring (March – early May):** Soil temperatures are too cold for warm-season germination.

## A Note on Front Range Rebates

Denver Water's Cash-for-Grass program pays a per-square-foot rebate for converting irrigated turf to water-wise landscaping. Visit denverwater.org/conservation/cash-for-grass for current rebate amounts — Denver Water updates rates annually based on conservation budget and demand. Don't trust dollar figures from third-party blogs that haven't been refreshed in a season.

Aurora Water, Castle Rock Water (ColoradoScape), and Centennial Water all run similar programs for their service areas. If your address is on the boundary between two water districts, look up the utility on your bill, not the city you live in. The water provider, not the municipality, runs the rebate. The CWCB statewide Turf Replacement Grant is paused as of March 2025; apply through your local utility.

The non-negotiable: **apply for pre-approval before removing any turf.** Every Front Range program disqualifies retroactive applications. Pre-approval takes 2–6 weeks depending on utility and time of year. Spring is the busiest application window; submit early.

After approval: demo to bare soil including the root zone, prep the seedbed, seed at the recommended rate, water through establishment, document with progress photos. Most Front Range programs require 60+ days of establishment before final inspection — longer for warm-season natives like Blue Grama and cold-hardy Buffalograss that develop slowly. Submit completion documentation (after-photos, seed receipts confirming species, certification forms), pass inspection, get paid in 4–8 weeks.
