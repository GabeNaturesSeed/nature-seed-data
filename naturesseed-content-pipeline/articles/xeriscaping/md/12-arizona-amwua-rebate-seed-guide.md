If you live in the Phoenix metro and you've been told there's "an AMWUA rebate" for ripping out your front lawn, the truth is more annoying than that. There is no single AMWUA rebate. AMWUA is a coalition — ten member water providers across central Arizona — and each member city runs its own turf-removal program with its own approved-plant list, its own application portal, and its own rebate rate. What Phoenix accepts, Scottsdale might handle differently. The fastest way to lose your conversion budget is to apply to the wrong program, demo your turf, and find out the city you're actually billed by uses different paperwork.

This guide breaks the AMWUA programs down city-by-city, calls out Tucson Water (which is not AMWUA and has a separate program), and lists the Nature's Seed species that work for low-desert Arizona conversions.

## What AMWUA Is — and Isn't

The Arizona Municipal Water Users Association is a 10-member coalition of water utilities across central Arizona. Member cities coordinate on conservation messaging, share data, and run aligned-but-not-identical programs. Member cities include: Phoenix, Mesa, Scottsdale, Tempe, Gilbert, Chandler, Glendale, Peoria, Surprise, and Avondale.

Importantly: **Tucson Water is NOT an AMWUA member.** Tucson runs its own conservation programs through its own utility, separate from the AMWUA coordination. If you live in Tucson, the Phoenix-area program details below don't apply to you — skip to the Tucson Water section further down.

Call or check each city's website for current rebate rates — AMWUA city programs update amounts regularly, and rates from a year-old blog post are not reliable. Some cities pay flat per-square-foot rates; some structure the rebate around tiered savings; some bundle landscape conversion with irrigation upgrades. Confirm the specific program structure for your service address before sizing the project.

A note on service-address vs. mailing-address: in the East Valley especially, mailing addresses can sit in one city while water service comes from another. Look up the utility name on your water bill, not the city on your driver's license, to figure out which program you apply to.

## Tucson Water — Separate Program

Tucson Water is the City of Tucson's utility and runs its own conservation programs independent of AMWUA. The relevant programs for landscape conversion:

- **Water Harvesting Incentive Program** — rebates for residential rainwater harvesting and graywater systems that support drought-resistant landscaping.
- **Rainwater Harvesting** — design guidance and incentives for passive water-harvesting earthworks.
- **Turf Reduction** — rebates for converting irrigated turf to xeriscape, similar in structure to AMWUA city programs but administered through Tucson Water directly.

Contact: tucsonaz.gov/water for current program details and rebate rates.

**Nature's Seed species for Tucson conversions:**

- [Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) — works for Tucson but consider whether you want lawn at all; many Tucson conversions go full xeric without turf substitute.
- [Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) — native to Arizona as well as Texas, structural bunchgrass appropriate for Tucson elevation.
- [Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix](/products/sonoran-desert-wildflower-mix/) — climate-appropriate seasonal color and pollinator support for Tucson's elevation and rainfall pattern.

## Nature's Seed Species for Low-Desert Arizona

The species below cover the AMWUA member cities (Phoenix metro at roughly 1,100 feet of elevation, hot summers, mild winters, low rainfall) and apply to most central Arizona programs.

### Common Bermudagrass

[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) is the standard lawn-replacement turf for the Phoenix metro and is approved by most AMWUA programs as a water-conserving turf alternative compared to higher-water cool-season grasses. Bermuda handles full Phoenix summer heat with minimal water once established, goes dormant tan in winter (which is the trade-off — overseeding with annual ryegrass for winter color is a separate decision), and establishes from seed in spring when soil temperatures hit the warm-season germination threshold.

For full lawn-style conversions where the homeowner wants to keep some kind of grass, Bermudagrass is the workhorse. For cities that want to see lower-water options, pair it with structural natives in border areas.

### Sideoats Grama

[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) is the Texas State Grass but is also native to Arizona — it ranges from Texas through the Southwest into northern Mexico — and qualifies as an approved native on most AMWUA program lists. It's a structural bunchgrass: clumping, with sideways-hanging seed heads, used as a feature planting in mixed-native designs rather than as a mowed turf substitute. Drought-adapted, slow to establish, and durable once mature.

### Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix

[Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix](/products/sonoran-desert-wildflower-mix/) is purpose-built for the Phoenix metro climate and the Sonoran Desert ecotone. Annual and perennial natives bring seasonal color through the cooler months, support pollinators (Sonoran Desert bees, hummingbirds, butterflies), and let you hit ground-cover thresholds in the parts of a converted area that aren't filled by Bermudagrass or structural natives.

Sow in fall (September–October) for late-winter and spring bloom; this is the standard low-desert wildflower window.

### Southwest Wildflower Mix

[Southwest Wildflower Mix](/products/southwest-wildflower-mix/) is the perennial-heavy companion mix. Where the Sonoran Desert mix carries more annuals for fast color, the Southwest mix provides longer-term groundcover from established perennials. Approved for ground cover in most AMWUA city programs and a good base mix for large converted areas where you want plantings that come back year over year without reseeding.

## Application Tips Specific to Arizona AMWUA Programs

Three patterns trip up Phoenix-metro applicants more than any others:

1. **Pre-approval is required — do NOT remove turf first.** Every AMWUA member city, and Tucson Water, requires you to submit the application and receive pre-approval before doing demo work. Demo performed before pre-approval forfeits the rebate. There is no retroactive path. If your contractor says, "we'll just rip it out and apply later," fire your contractor.

2. **60-day establishment window before final inspection.** Most AMWUA programs require at least 60 days between completion of the conversion and final inspection. This gives seeded conversions time to germinate and reach the program's required ground-cover threshold (typically 60–70%). Don't schedule the inspection too early; an inspection that fails because cover is incomplete sends you back to the start of the documentation cycle.

3. **Bring or upload seed bag labels at completion.** The inspectors verify species at inspection time. With sod, the species is visible. With seed, the inspector needs documentation — the seed bag tag with the species name, the receipt from your seed supplier, or a photo of the bag uploaded to the application portal. Save your seed bags. Photograph the labels before you throw them out.

The most common rejection reason at final inspection: plant cover below the program's threshold. Cities typically require 60–70% live ground cover at inspection time. If your seeding hasn't filled in (germination was patchy, irrigation was uneven, the dog dug a trench across the yard), the inspector will reject and require a re-inspection after additional establishment. Avoid this by giving seeded conversions a full 60–90 days of establishment, watering consistently through the first growing season, and overseeding any thin patches before the inspection date.

## Seeding Windows for AMWUA Cities (Phoenix Metro)

- **Spring (March 15 – April 30):** Primary window for warm-season grasses, especially Bermudagrass. Soil is warm enough for germination, daytime temperatures are mild enough not to fry seedlings, and seedlings have the full warm season to establish before winter dormancy.
- **Fall (September 1 – October 15):** Primary window for native grasses and wildflower mixes. Cooler nighttime temperatures and the (modest) fall rainfall pattern give natives a head start. The Sonoran Desert Wildflower Mix is fall-sown specifically; spring sowing of cold-season annual wildflowers is too late to establish before summer heat.
- **Avoid summer seeding (May 15 – August 31):** Phoenix summer heat shuts down germination for most species and bakes seedlings before they develop a root system. Even Bermudagrass — heat-tolerant once established — has a hard time germinating reliably in mid-summer Phoenix.

## Putting It Together

Pick the right city program for your service address, get pre-approval before you touch the turf, plant climate-appropriate species in the right window, document the establishment, and pass inspection at the 60-day mark. The rebate clears in 4–8 weeks after passing.
