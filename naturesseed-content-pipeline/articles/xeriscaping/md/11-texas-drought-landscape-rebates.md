Texas does not have a statewide turf rebate program. What it has, instead, is a patchwork of city-run incentives — San Antonio, Austin, El Paso, and a handful of smaller utilities each running their own program with their own rules — plus one statewide law that does more for drought-resistant landscaping than any single rebate ever did. That law is Texas Property Code § 202.007, and if you live in an HOA neighborhood, it's the difference between hoping your covenants will let you xeriscape and knowing they have to.

This guide covers both: the city-by-city rebate landscape and the statewide HOA protection that lets you collect the rebate without getting a violation letter from your homeowners association the next week.

## Texas Property Code § 202.007: HOAs Cannot Block Drought-Resistant Landscaping

Texas Property Code § 202.007 prohibits homeowners associations from enforcing any covenant, restriction, or rule that prevents an owner from installing drought-resistant landscaping or water-conserving turf. The statute names "drought-resistant landscaping" and "water-conserving natural turf" specifically as protected categories — meaning the HOA cannot fine you, force you to remove it, or require you to revert to a higher-water lawn even if the original CC&Rs say you must maintain "lush green grass" or "no more than 25% non-turf groundcover."

The statute does carve out room for HOAs to require reasonable design standards. They can ask for a written landscape plan, require defined edges and clean transitions, and set guidelines for maintenance. What they can't do is say no to the underlying concept. A blanket prohibition on Buffalograss, native wildflower areas, or Bermudagrass-replacement seed is unenforceable in Texas regardless of what the deed restrictions say.

Practically, this means you can convert a Kentucky-bluegrass-style St. Augustine front yard to [Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/), [Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/), or a [Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/), and the HOA cannot enforce a violation. The standard playbook when an HOA pushes back: cite § 202.007 in writing, submit a landscape plan with species names and a maintenance schedule, and reference the statute in your design review packet. Most HOA boards will green-light the application once they see the legal cite. The ones that don't lose in mediation.

## SAWS WaterSaver Landscape (San Antonio)

The San Antonio Water System (SAWS) WaterSaver program is the gold standard for Texas turf rebates. It pays a per-square-foot rebate for converting irrigated turf to drought-resistant landscaping that meets specific design criteria — typically a mix of native plants, mulched beds, and turf-replacement species. Visit saws.org/conservation for current rebate amounts and program details; SAWS adjusts rates annually and the per-square-foot value depends on the conversion type.

**Seed qualifies — but the documentation is stricter than sod.** SAWS will accept a seeded conversion, but you have to document the establishment. That means germination photos, progress photos at the 30-day mark, and an established-cover photo showing at least 70% ground cover before final inspection. With sod, the inspector arrives and sees a finished lawn; with seed, the inspector needs evidence that the seeded area actually filled in.

**Nature's Seed species that qualify in San Antonio:**

- [Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) — the workhorse warm-season turf for Central Texas. Establishes fast from seed in May and June, drought-tolerant once established, accepted as a water-conserving turf alternative for higher-water cool-season conversions.
- [Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) — the native option. Slower to establish, lower water requirements once mature, and appropriate for full-sun areas with good drainage.
- [Texas Native Grass Mix](/products/texas-native-lawn-mix/) — a blend designed for drought-tolerant lawn-style conversions in Texas. Mix-of-natives reads as intentional landscape design and meets SAWS native-species preferences.

## Austin Water Smart Conservation Rebates

The Austin Water utility runs landscape-conversion incentives under its broader conservation program. Visit austintexas.gov/austinwater for current rates and specific program names — Austin has shifted program structure several times in the last decade and the current incentive may bundle landscape conversion with irrigation upgrades. Confirm the active program before sizing your project.

The application flow is similar to SAWS: pre-approval, demo, replant, document, inspect, get paid. Austin's design preferences lean toward native and adapted plants with documented water savings; pure ornamental conversions face more scrutiny than functional native landscape designs.

**Nature's Seed species that work in Austin:**

- [Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) — the strong performer for Austin's full-sun front yards. Native to the Edwards Plateau and points west, well-adapted to Central Texas summers, and accepted by Austin Water as a turf-replacement species.
- [Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/) — for ornamental-area conversions, pollinator strips, and the bands of color around structural plantings. Austin's program documentation often references native wildflower areas favorably.

## El Paso Water Utilities (EPWU)

El Paso runs the Xeriscape Landscape Conversion incentive program under EPWU. Visit epwu.org for current rebate rates and approved-plant lists. El Paso's climate is more like West Texas and southern Arizona than like Central Texas — a true Chihuahuan Desert climate with low rainfall, intense summer heat, and mild winters — so the species recommendations are different.

**Nature's Seed species that work in El Paso:**

- [Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) — appropriate for the El Paso climate where summer heat is the dominant constraint. Establishes fast and tolerates the heat better than most cool-season species.
- [Blue Grama](/products/blue-grama/) — the native short-grass option. Native to the Chihuahuan Desert ecotone and well-suited to El Paso's elevation (3,800 feet) and climate.

If you're in El Paso and reading the SAWS or Austin Water sections, ignore them — Central Texas species recommendations don't translate well to the Chihuahuan Desert. Stick with the West Texas / southern Arizona playbook.

## "Can I Use Seed Instead of Sod?"

Short answer: yes, for SAWS and most Texas city rebate programs. Long answer: it's worth doing for larger conversions but requires more documentation discipline.

The trade-offs:

- **Sod is simpler to document.** The inspector arrives, sees an established lawn, and signs off. Establishment is essentially instant from a verification standpoint.
- **Seed is cheaper, especially at scale.** A 5,000-square-foot front yard might be $4,000–$6,000 in sod versus a fraction of that in seed. The difference more than pays for the extra documentation work.
- **Seed gives you native and water-saving species that aren't readily available in sod form.** Buffalograss is sometimes available as sod but at premium prices; native wildflower mixes are seed-only.
- **Documentation requirement:** germination photos at 14–21 days, progress photos at 30 days, and established-cover photos at 60–90 days. Time-stamp your phone photos. Save your seed receipts and the bag tags for species verification at inspection.

If you're seeding more than 2,000 square feet and have the patience to wait through the establishment window, seed is almost always the better economic call.

## Sideoats Grama — The Texas State Grass

[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) is the official state grass of Texas and qualifies for most Texas water utility rebate programs as a native species. It's a structural bunchgrass — clumping, with characteristic sideways-hanging seed heads — that works well as a feature planting, in mixed-native plantings, and as part of a converted area where you want some vertical texture. It's not a turf substitute; you don't mow it and walk on it like a lawn. But for the prairie-style or naturalistic portion of a SAWS or Austin Water conversion, Sideoats Grama is a reliable choice.

## Texas Bluebonnets and Seasonal Color

[Texas Bluebonnet Seeds](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/) belong in any xeriscape design as seasonal companion planting alongside native grasses. Bluebonnets are state-iconic, drought-adapted, and reseed themselves year over year once established. They flower in early spring before warm-season grasses fully green up — adding color to the converted area during the awkward shoulder season when Buffalograss is still tan from winter dormancy.

Sow bluebonnets in fall (September–November) for spring bloom. They overlap well with native wildflower mixes and look intentional rather than weedy when planted in defined bands or drifts within a mixed-native conversion.

## Putting It Together

The Texas approach to drought-resistant landscaping has two legs. The state law (§ 202.007) gives you the right to install it. The city rebate programs give you money for installing it. Use both.
