Texas does not have a statewide turf rebate. What it has, instead, is a patchwork of city-run incentives — San Antonio, Austin, El Paso, and a handful of smaller utilities each running their own program — plus one state law (Texas Property Code § 202.007) that prevents HOAs from blocking drought-resistant landscaping. The combination matters: the law gives you the right to plant, the rebates help cover the cost, and the seed below is what actually thrives in Texas climate zones.

This guide leads with the seed. The rebate paperwork and the HOA shield are at the bottom.

## Texas Seed Lineup by Climate Zone

### Hill Country / Central Texas (Austin, San Antonio Corridor)

Alkaline soils, often shallow over limestone. Hot dry summers, mild winters, annual rainfall 28–34 inches.

#### Buffalograss — the Hill Country lawn

[Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) is the native go-to for Hill Country lawns. It's adapted to alkaline limestone-derived soils — the soils that fight St. Augustine — and runs on a fraction of the water once established. Soft, low, silvery-green when active, dormant tan November–March. Mature unmowed height: 4–8 inches.

Why it works here: native to the Edwards Plateau and points west, evolved through the same drought cycles that define Central Texas. Approved by SAWS and Austin Water as a turf-replacement species.

#### Sideoats Grama — the Texas State Grass

[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) is the official state grass of Texas, designated by the 67th Legislature in 1971. Warm-season native bunchgrass, 18–30 inches tall, with the distinctive flag-like seed heads that hang off one side of the stem. Adapted to every region of Texas. Qualifies on every Texas city rebate program as a native species.

It's not a turf substitute — you don't mow it and walk on it. It's the structural element along fence lines, driveways, slopes, and as the "tall" element in a meadow-style front yard. Putting the state grass in your front yard is also one of the easiest HOA conversations to have.

Order Sideoats Grama at [Nature's Seed](/products/sideoats-grama/).

#### Texas Native Wildflower Mix and Texas Native Grass Mix

[Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/) is the standard Hill Country meadow blend — bluebonnets, Indian paintbrush, gaillardia, coreopsis, Mexican hat. Plant in fall for spring through summer color across multiple species. Austin Water and SAWS programs reference native wildflower areas favorably.

[Texas Native Grass Mix](/products/texas-native-lawn-mix/) is a blend designed for drought-tolerant lawn-style conversions. Mix-of-natives reads as intentional landscape design and meets SAWS native-species preferences.

Order Texas Native Wildflower Mix at [Nature's Seed](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/) and Texas Native Grass Mix at [Nature's Seed](/products/texas-native-lawn-mix/).

#### Texas Bluebonnet Seeds — spring color

[Texas Bluebonnet Seeds](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/) are state-iconic, drought-adapted, and reseed themselves year over year once established. They flower in early spring before warm-season grasses fully green up — adding color during the awkward shoulder season when Buffalograss is still tan.

Sow in fall (September–November) for spring bloom. They overlap well with native wildflower mixes and look intentional rather than weedy when planted in defined bands or drifts.

The Hill Country list. Austin Water's design preferences lean toward native and adapted plants with documented water savings. ### West Texas / El Paso (EPWU service area)

Chihuahuan Desert climate — true desert with low rainfall, intense summer heat, mild winters. Different species set than Central Texas.

#### Common Bermudagrass — heat-adapted lawn

[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) is appropriate for the El Paso climate where summer heat is the dominant constraint. Establishes fast and tolerates the heat better than most cool-season species.

#### Blue Grama — native short-grass option

[Blue Grama](/products/blue-grama/) is native to the Chihuahuan Desert ecotone and well-suited to El Paso's elevation (3,800 feet) and climate. The fine-bladed eyebrow-headed prairie grass that defines the Western desert. Order at [Nature's Seed](/products/blue-grama/).

If you're in El Paso and reading the SAWS or Austin Water sections, ignore them — Central Texas species recommendations don't translate well to the Chihuahuan Desert. Stick with the West Texas / southern Arizona playbook.

## Texas Property Code § 202.007: HOAs Cannot Block Drought-Resistant Landscaping

Texas Property Code § 202.007 prohibits homeowners associations from enforcing any covenant, restriction, or rule that prevents an owner from installing drought-resistant landscaping or water-conserving turf. The statute names "drought-resistant landscaping" and "water-conserving natural turf" specifically as protected categories.

The HOA can ask for a written landscape plan, require defined edges and clean transitions, and set guidelines for maintenance. They cannot say no to the underlying concept. A blanket prohibition on Buffalograss, native wildflower areas, or Bermudagrass-replacement seed is unenforceable in Texas regardless of what the deed restrictions say.

Standard playbook when an HOA pushes back: cite § 202.007 in writing, submit a landscape plan with species names and a maintenance schedule, reference the statute in your design review packet. Most boards green-light the application once they see the legal cite.

## A Note on Texas Local Rebates

### SAWS WaterSaver (San Antonio)

SAWS pays a per-square-foot rebate for converting irrigated turf to drought-resistant landscaping. The program is structured as a coupon redeemable for plants, mulch, and irrigation supplies at participating nurseries — reimbursement of materials rather than a per-square-foot cash check. Visit saws.org/conservation for current rates. Seed qualifies but documentation is stricter than sod (germination photos, progress photos, established-cover photos showing 70% ground cover).

### Austin Water Smart

Austin Water runs landscape-conversion incentives under its broader conservation program. Visit austintexas.gov/austinwater for current rates — Austin has shifted program structure several times, and the current incentive may bundle landscape conversion with irrigation upgrades. Pre-approval, demo, replant, document, inspect.

### El Paso Water Utilities (EPWU)

EPWU runs the Xeriscape Landscape Conversion incentive program. Visit epwu.org for current rebate rates and approved-plant lists.

The non-negotiable across all three programs: **apply for pre-approval before removing any turf.** Demoing first forfeits the rebate. Pre-approval, demo, replant, document with timestamped photos, inspection, payment.

For mid-size Texas cities not listed (Fort Worth, Houston, Lubbock, Corpus Christi, McAllen, Amarillo) — call your water utility's conservation office directly. Many have small conservation programs that come and go with budget cycles.

## "Can I Use Seed Instead of Sod?"

Short answer: yes, for SAWS and most Texas city rebate programs. Trade-offs:

- **Sod is simpler to document** but materially expensive. A 5,000-square-foot front yard might be $4,000–$6,000 in sod versus a fraction of that in seed.
- **Seed is cheaper at scale** and gives you native species (Buffalograss, native wildflower mixes) that aren't readily available in sod form.
- **Documentation requirement for seed:** germination photos at 14–21 days, progress photos at 30 days, established-cover photos at 60–90 days. Save your seed receipts and bag tags for species verification at inspection.

If you're seeding more than 2,000 square feet and have the patience to wait through the establishment window, seed is almost always the better economic call.
