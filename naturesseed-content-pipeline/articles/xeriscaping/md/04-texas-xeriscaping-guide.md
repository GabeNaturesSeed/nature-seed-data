The letter shows up in your mailbox in late June. The Hill Country has been over 100°F for three weeks, the city's been on Stage 2 watering restrictions since April, and the front lawn — the one your HOA's CC&Rs require you to keep "lush green" — is brown. The letter is a violation notice. Fix the lawn within 30 days or pay $200 a month in fines.

Two things are true at once. First: that letter is unenforceable. Texas Property Code § 202.007 prohibits HOAs from blocking drought-resistant landscaping, period. Second: the St. Augustine the previous owner planted is the wrong grass for Texas. It always was. The fix is replacing it with seed that's actually adapted to your climate zone — Buffalograss in the Hill Country, Bermudagrass on the Gulf Coast, Blue Grama in West Texas — and Texas has more native lawn options than just about any state in the country.

This guide covers the species that work in each Texas climate zone, the legal protection that lets you plant them without fighting the HOA, and where to order each one.

## Sideoats Grama: The Texas State Grass

Designated by the 67th Texas Legislature in 1971, [Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) (*Bouteloua curtipendula*) is the official state grass of Texas. It's a warm-season native bunchgrass that grows 18–30 inches tall, with the distinctive "side oats" — small spike flowers that hang off one side of the stem in late summer, turning purple-bronze in fall.

What makes it work in Texas: it's adapted to every region of the state from the Hill Country to the Trans-Pecos to the Panhandle. Tolerates poor soils, alkalinity, drought, and the heat. Once established it runs on rainfall in most years. As an HOA conversation tool, putting the state grass in your front yard is hard to argue against.

In a xeriscape design, Sideoats Grama works as a structural accent grass along fence lines, the "tall" element in a meadow-style front yard mixed with shorter grasses and wildflowers, a native alternative to ornamental Pampas grass, and erosion control on Hill Country slopes that won't hold turf.

## Texas Bluebonnets: Spring Color Anchor

[Texas Bluebonnet Seeds](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/) (*Lupinus texensis*) punch above their weight emotionally and visually in any Texas landscape design. Bluebonnets are an annual cool-season wildflower that needs cold winter stratification to germinate properly. Plant the seeds in **September or October** for spring bloom — they emerge in late winter, bloom in late March through April, and set seed before dying off in May/June heat. The bare ground left behind is then perfect for warm-season natives (Sideoats Grama, Buffalograss) to fill in for the rest of the year.

Why bluebonnets work in Texas: they're nitrogen-fixing legumes that fertilize themselves and nearby plantings, drought-adapted across the state, and self-reseeding once established — the patch expands every year on its own. Tips for successful establishment:

- Scarify the seed if your supplier hasn't already (light sandpaper rub on the hard seed coat)
- Plant in well-drained soil — they hate wet feet
- Don't fertilize. Over-fertilization produces leaves at the expense of flowers
- Plant alongside native grasses for a year-round cover system

Order Texas Bluebonnet Seeds at [Nature's Seed](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/) — ships to your door.

A bluebonnet + Sideoats Grama + Buffalograss combination in a Hill Country front yard delivers spring color (March–April), summer green (Buffalograss + Sideoats Grama active), fall structural interest (Sideoats Grama seed heads turning bronze), and dormant tan in winter (acceptable in Texas; matches the surrounding hill landscape). Total water input: about 25% of what a St. Augustine lawn demands.

## Regional Breakdown: What Grows Where in Texas

Texas covers more climate zones than any other state — Gulf Coast humidity in Houston, Hill Country alkaline rocky soil in Austin, North Texas blackland prairie clay in Dallas, West Texas alkaline desert in El Paso. The seed strategy varies by zone.

### Hill Country / Central TX (Austin, San Antonio Corridor)

Alkaline soils, often shallow over limestone. Hot dry summers, mild winters. Annual rainfall 28–34 inches but increasingly unreliable.

#### Buffalograss — the Hill Country lawn

[Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) is the native go-to for Hill Country lawns. It's adapted to alkaline limestone-derived soils — the soils that fight St. Augustine — and runs on a fraction of the water once established. Soft, low, silvery-green when active, dormant tan November–March. Mature unmowed height: 4–8 inches, so you can mow rarely or not at all.

### North TX (DFW Metro)

Blackland prairie soil — heavy clay. Hot summers, occasional ice storms. Annual rainfall 36–40 inches with brutal summer dry spells.

[Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/) is native to the blackland prairie — it's what was here before St. Augustine and Bermuda took over — and handles clay soil better than most warm-season grasses.

[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) works for south-facing slopes, high-traffic areas, and properties where the homeowner wants more turf-like density. Bermuda handles DFW clay well once established and recovers from foot traffic faster than buffalograss.

[Texas Bluebonnet Seeds](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/) for spring color and [Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/) for ongoing seasonal interest.

### Gulf Coast (Houston, Corpus Christi, Beaumont)

High humidity, intense rainfall events, hot summers, mild winters. Annual rainfall 40–55 inches but concentrated in storm events. Soils often poorly drained or sandy.

[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) handles humidity and heat — the default Gulf Coast lawn replacement when homeowners want active turf. Triblade Elite is the upgrade option: see [Triblade Elite Bermudagrass Lawn Mix](/products/triblade-elite-bermudagrass-lawn-mix/) for a finer, denser finished look.

[Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/) for seasonal color in beds, parkways, and edges. Many of the species in the mix are coastal-adapted.

### West TX / El Paso / Trans-Pecos

Desert conditions. Annual rainfall under 12 inches in El Paso. Alkaline soils, intense UV, big day-night temperature swings. Climate similar to neighboring New Mexico and Arizona.

[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/) is tolerant of the heat and alkaline soils and the right choice for any El Paso lawn.

[Blue Grama](/products/blue-grama/) is the native short grass of the high plains and West Texas. Handles the freeze cycles that get worse as you move toward the Panhandle and away from the Gulf. Order at [Nature's Seed](/products/blue-grama/).

[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) as accent. Wildflowers from desert-adapted mixes — West Texas native palettes overlap heavily with New Mexico and southern Colorado.

## When to Seed in Texas

Texas timing windows are wider than the high-desert states because of milder winters, but still narrow enough that missing them costs you a season.

**Hill Country and North Texas:**
- Spring: March 15 – May 15 for warm-season grasses
- Fall: September 1 – October 30
- Bluebonnets: September 1 – October 15 (must be planted in fall)

**Gulf Coast:**
- Spring: March 1 – May 31
- Fall: September 15 – November 15
- Wildflowers: October–November

**West Texas / El Paso:**
- Same timing as low-desert Arizona — March 15 – April 30 spring, September 1 – October 15 fall

## Texas Property Code § 202.007: Your Legal Shield

The relevant statute is short and direct. Property Code § 202.007(a) states:

> A property owners' association may not include or enforce a provision in a dedicatory instrument that prohibits or restricts a property owner from . . . using drought-resistant landscaping or water-conserving turf.

The HOA *can* establish reasonable design standards for the appearance and maintenance of drought-resistant landscaping. They cannot prohibit it outright, and they cannot enforce existing CC&R provisions that would block it.

In practice, this means:
- You can replace your front-yard turf with Buffalograss, Bermudagrass, or native wildflowers without needing HOA approval to "switch grass types"
- The HOA cannot fine you for non-green dormant grass during state-declared drought conditions
- The HOA cannot require you to maintain or install St. Augustine, Kentucky bluegrass, or other high-water turf species

If you're getting fined: request a copy of the specific CC&R provision being enforced, send a written response citing § 202.007, submit a complete xeriscape plan that demonstrates reasonable design — clean edges, defined plant beds, irrigation included, plant list. Most HOAs back down at step 1 or 2.

## A Note on Texas Local Rebates

Texas doesn't have a statewide turf removal rebate. Each major city utility runs its own program: SAWS in San Antonio (~$1.00/sq ft via the WaterSaver coupon), Austin Water (~$0.35/sq ft up to $3,000), El Paso Water Utilities (~$0.50/sq ft), with smaller programs in DFW. Rates change year to year.

Apply *before* removing any turf — every Texas program disqualifies retroactive applications. The [Texas drought landscape rebates](/texas-drought-landscape-rebates/) guide breaks down each city program and the documentation required for seed-path applications.
