The letter shows up in your mailbox in late June. The Hill Country has been over 100°F for three weeks, the city's been on Stage 2 watering restrictions since April, and the front lawn — the one your HOA's CC&Rs require you to keep "lush green" — is brown. The letter is a violation notice. Fix the lawn within 30 days or pay $200 a month in fines.

This is happening to Texas homeowners every summer, and most of them don't know that the letter is unenforceable. Texas state law — Property Code § 202.007 — prohibits HOAs from blocking drought-resistant landscaping. Your HOA can ask you to maintain a yard. They cannot legally fine you for replacing turf with native grasses, wildflowers, or other water-efficient species.

The HOAs know this. The board members often don't. The management companies sometimes know but find it easier to send the same form letter every June rather than rewrite the CC&Rs.

If you've gotten one of these letters — or you're tired of paying $300 a month in summer water bills to keep the kind of lawn that survives in Atlanta but not in Austin — this guide is the answer.

## Texas Property Code § 202.007: Your Legal Shield

The relevant statute is short and direct. Property Code § 202.007(a) states:

> A property owners' association may not include or enforce a provision in a dedicatory instrument that prohibits or restricts a property owner from . . . using drought-resistant landscaping or water-conserving turf.

The statute goes on to clarify what the HOA *can* do: they can establish reasonable design standards for the appearance and maintenance of drought-resistant landscaping. They cannot prohibit it outright, and they cannot enforce existing CC&R provisions that would block it.

In practice, this means:
- You can replace your front-yard turf with Buffalograss, Bermudagrass, or native wildflowers without needing HOA approval to "switch grass types"
- You can submit a xeriscape design plan to the HOA's architectural review committee, but the HOA must approve any design that meets reasonable aesthetic standards using drought-resistant species
- The HOA cannot fine you for non-green dormant grass during state-declared drought conditions
- The HOA cannot require you to maintain or install St. Augustine, Kentucky bluegrass, or other high-water turf species

If you're getting fined, the playbook is:
1. Request a copy of the specific CC&R provision being enforced
2. Send a written response citing § 202.007
3. Submit a complete xeriscape plan that demonstrates reasonable design — clean edges, defined plant beds, irrigation included, plant list from a recognized native species reference
4. If they still don't back down, the Texas Attorney General has issued multiple opinions supporting homeowner xeriscape rights, and Texas courts have consistently ruled in favor of homeowners

Most HOAs back down at step 1 or 2. The few that don't are exposed to legal challenge they almost always lose.

## Sideoats Grama: The Texas State Grass

Designated by the 67th Texas Legislature in 1971, Sideoats Grama (*Bouteloua curtipendula*) is the official state grass of Texas. Putting it in your front yard isn't a hippie act. It's a Texas pride act.

[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/) is a warm-season native bunchgrass that grows 18–30 inches tall, with the distinctive "side oats" — small spike flowers that hang off one side of the stem in late summer, turning purple-bronze in fall. It's adapted to every region of Texas from the Hill Country to the Trans-Pecos to the Panhandle, tolerates poor soils, handles drought better than just about any other ornamental grass, and provides cover and seed for native wildlife.

In a xeriscape design, Sideoats Grama works as:
- A structural accent grass along fence lines and driveways
- The "tall" element in a meadow-style front yard mixed with shorter grasses and wildflowers
- A native alternative to Pampas grass or Maiden grass for ornamental clumps near patios
- Erosion control on Hill Country slopes that won't hold turf

It's also one of the easiest natives to establish from seed. Plant in spring (March–April) or fall (September–October), water through establishment, and it's set for a decade with almost no input.

If you're going to put one native plant in your yard to make the xeriscape conversation easier with your HOA, pick the state grass. You're not "letting your yard go" — you're planting Texas's official grass species. The framing matters.

## Bluebonnets: The Other Texas Pride Anchor

[Texas Bluebonnet Seeds](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/) (*Lupinus texensis*) need their own section because they punch above their weight emotionally and visually in any Texas landscape design.

Bluebonnets are an annual cool-season wildflower. Plant the seeds in **September or October** for spring bloom — they need cold winter conditions to germinate properly. The plants emerge in late winter, bloom in late March through April, and set seed before dying off in May/June heat. The bare ground left behind is then perfect for warm-season native grasses (Sideoats Grama, Buffalograss) to fill in for the rest of the year.

Tips for successful bluebonnet establishment:
- Scarify the seed if your supplier hasn't already (light sandpaper rub on hard seed coat)
- Plant in well-drained soil — they hate wet feet
- Don't fertilize. Bluebonnets fix their own nitrogen and over-fertilization produces leaves at the expense of flowers
- Plant alongside native grasses for a year-round cover system
- Once established, bluebonnets reseed naturally and the patch expands every year

A bluebonnet + Sideoats Grama + Buffalograss combination in a Hill Country front yard creates a yard that has:
- Spring color (March–April bluebonnets)
- Summer green (Buffalograss + Sideoats Grama active)
- Fall structural interest (Sideoats Grama seed heads turning bronze)
- Winter dormant tan (acceptable in Texas; matches surrounding hill landscape)

Total water input: about 25% of what a St. Augustine lawn demands.

## Regional Breakdown: What Grows Where in Texas

Texas covers more climate zones than any other state — Gulf Coast humidity in Houston, Hill Country alkaline rocky soil in Austin, North Texas blackland prairie clay in Dallas, West Texas alkaline desert in El Paso. The seed strategy varies.

### Hill Country / Central TX (Austin, San Antonio Corridor)

Alkaline soils, often shallow over limestone. Hot dry summers, mild winters. Annual rainfall 28–34 inches but increasingly unreliable. Drought is the default condition for half the year.

**Recommended species:**
- **[Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/)** — The native go-to for Hill Country lawns. Soft, low, silvery-green when active, dormant brown November–March. Once established, asks for almost nothing. Particularly well-adapted to alkaline limestone-derived soils.
- **[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/)** — As accent and structural grass. The state grass connection is a useful HOA conversation tool.
- **[Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/)** — Bluebonnets, Indian paintbrush, gaillardia, coreopsis, Mexican hat. Standard Hill Country meadow components.
- **[Texas Native Grass Mix](/products/texas-native-lawn-mix/)** — A blend of Texas-adapted native grasses for homeowners who want a more "lawn-like" look without committing to one species.

### North TX (DFW Metro)

Blackland prairie soil — heavy clay. Hot summers, occasional ice storms. Annual rainfall 36–40 inches but with brutal summer dry spells.

**Recommended species:**
- **[Buffalograss](/products/buffalograss/)** — Native to the blackland prairie, this is what was here before St. Augustine and Bermuda took over. Handles clay soil better than most warm-season grasses.
- **[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/)** — For south-facing slopes, high-traffic areas, and properties where the homeowner wants more turf-like density. Bermuda handles DFW clay well once established.
- **[Texas Bluebonnet Seeds](/products/texas-bluebonnet-seeds/)** for spring color
- **[Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/)** for ongoing seasonal interest

### Gulf Coast (Houston, Corpus Christi, Beaumont)

High humidity, intense rainfall events, hot summers, mild winters. Annual rainfall 40–55 inches but concentrated in storm events. Soils often poorly drained or sandy.

**Recommended species:**
- **[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/)** — Handles humidity and heat. The default Gulf Coast lawn replacement when homeowners want active turf.
- **[Texas Native Wildflower Mix](/products/texas-native-wildflower-mix/)** — For seasonal color in beds, parkways, and edges. Many of the species are coastal-adapted.
- Native warm-season bunchgrasses (Gulf muhly, Gulf cordgrass) are good Gulf Coast accents — available at native plant nurseries and farm co-ops if not stocked locally.

Sideoats Grama works on the Gulf Coast but is less suited to the heaviest humidity zones near the actual coastline.

### West TX / El Paso / Trans-Pecos

Desert conditions. Annual rainfall under 12 inches in El Paso. Alkaline soils, intense UV, big day-night temperature swings. Climate similar to neighboring New Mexico and Arizona.

**Recommended species:**
- **[Common Bermudagrass](/products/common-bermudagrass/)** — Tolerant of the heat and alkaline soils.
- **[Blue Grama](/products/blue-grama/)** — Native to the high plains and West Texas. Handles the freeze cycles that get worse as you move toward the Panhandle and away from the Gulf.
- **[Sideoats Grama](/products/sideoats-grama/)** as accent
- Wildflowers from desert-adapted mixes (West Texas native mixes overlap heavily with New Mexico and southern Colorado palettes)

## Local Rebate Programs

Texas doesn't have statewide turf removal rebates the way California and Nevada do. Each major water utility runs its own program, and rates vary widely.

### San Antonio Water System (SAWS) — WaterSaver Landscape Coupon

SAWS pays up to **$100 per 100 sq ft** of converted landscape (effectively $1.00/sq ft) up to a $400 cap for residential. The program is structured as a coupon redeemable for plants, mulch, and irrigation supplies at participating nurseries — it's reimbursement of materials rather than a per-square-foot cash check.

Process: take a free SAWS landscape design class, submit a plan, get pre-approved, demo and install, submit photos and receipts, get the coupon credited.

### Austin Water Smart — WaterWise Landscape Rebate

Austin Water pays up to **$3,000** for residential turf-to-WaterWise conversions, structured as $35 per 100 sq ft (effectively $0.35/sq ft). Application process includes a pre-conversion site visit and a post-conversion inspection.

### El Paso Water Utilities (EPWU) — TimeToXeriscape Rebate

EPWU pays up to **$0.50/sq ft** for turf removal, capped per residential property. El Paso has run xeriscape rebates since the 1990s and the application process is more streamlined than in newer programs.

### Other Programs

- Dallas Water Utilities runs limited rebate programs through specific neighborhoods
- Houston has educational programs but no general rebate
- Smaller districts (NTMWD, Tarrant Regional, etc.) sometimes offer pass-through programs through member cities

The [Texas drought landscape rebates](/texas-drought-landscape-rebates/) guide breaks down each program's application paperwork and the documentation required.

## When to Seed in Texas

Texas timing windows are wider than the high-desert states because of milder winters, but still narrow enough that missing them costs you a season.

**Hill Country and North Texas:**
- Spring: March 15 – May 15 for warm-season grasses
- Fall: September 1 – October 30
- Bluebonnets: September 1 – October 15 (must be planted in fall)

**Gulf Coast:**
- Spring: March 1 – May 31
- Fall: September 15 – November 15
- Wildflowers: October–November

**West Texas / El Paso:**
- Same timing as low-desert Arizona — March 15 – April 30 spring, September 1 – October 15 fall

## What a Texas Xeriscape Conversion Actually Looks Like

A 3,000 sq ft Austin Hill Country front yard converted from St. Augustine to a Buffalograss + Sideoats Grama + Texas Native Wildflower Mix system:

- Buffalograss (1,500 sq ft active lawn): ~$180 in seed
- Sideoats Grama (500 sq ft accent): ~$60 in seed
- Texas Native Wildflower Mix (500 sq ft beds): ~$80 in seed
- Texas Bluebonnet Seeds (500 sq ft front border): ~$50
- Drip irrigation conversion: ~$500
- Mulch and edging: ~$300
- Total: ~$1,170
- Austin Water rebate: 3,000 × $0.35 = $1,050
- Net out of pocket: ~$120
- Annual water savings: ~$800–$1,500

That's almost-free conversion year one and meaningful savings every year after. And the result is a yard that uses Texas natives, blooms with bluebonnets in spring, supports pollinators, and won't get HOA letters because § 202.007 protects the design.
